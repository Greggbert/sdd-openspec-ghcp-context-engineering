16  Making SDD a Team Practice

16.1 Learning objective

Establish a proportionate, reviewable OpenSpec workflow that can survive beyond the book’s exercises.

The exercises used one person, two repositories, and a controlled sequence of changes. A team has to answer harder questions: who owns a decision, how much specification work a change deserves, and how a reviewer can recover the reasoning after the original Copilot session is gone. SDD becomes a team practice when the repository records those decisions without turning every edit into a formal project.

16.2 Put decisions in the right place

An agent can propose code, tests, and artifact text. It cannot decide whether a business behavior is acceptable, whether a contract change is compatible with existing clients, or whether a risk is worth the cost. A human owner must make those decisions and leave enough evidence for another person to review them.

Use the repository artifacts for engineering intent and Jira for work tracking:

  • Jira records the work item, status, priority, and responsible people.
  • proposal.md records the business goal, scope, and non-goals for a change.
  • Delta specifications record the behavior the team will add, modify, or remove.
  • design.md records implementation decisions and their constraints.
  • tasks.md records the work sequence and its verification points.
  • Commits, pull requests, and test output provide evidence that the accepted intent was implemented.

This division prevents two common failures. A Jira ticket should not be the only place where an agent can find a behavioral rule, and an OpenSpec change should not become a second work-tracking system.

16.3 Make traceability recoverable

Traceability is useful when it lets a reviewer move from a decision to its evidence without asking the original author to reconstruct the conversation. Define a small naming and linking convention for both repositories. For example, a Jira issue can link to the OpenSpec change name, the pull request can mention the same issue, and the verification record can point to the relevant test command or result.

The exact convention matters less than using it consistently. At minimum, a reviewer should be able to answer these questions:

  • Which business decision does this change implement?
  • Which requirements and scenarios express that decision?
  • Which code, generated artifacts, and tests carry it out?
  • What evidence supports synchronization and archive readiness?

Keep the links close to the artifacts they explain. A change that affects the backend contract and the Angular client should identify both repositories and the point at which the client must be regenerated. That makes the contract boundary visible during review instead of leaving it implicit in task order.

16.4 Assign review ownership

Review ownership follows the decision being reviewed. The same person may hold several roles on a small change, but the roles should still be explicit.

  • A product or business owner reviews behavior, scope, and non-goals when the change affects what the system promises.
  • A technical reviewer checks contracts, architecture, data handling, and integration boundaries.
  • An implementation reviewer checks that code and tests satisfy the reviewed requirements.
  • The person responsible for synchronization or archival checks that the accepted specifications and evidence are complete.

These reviews do not require four people or four meetings. They require the team to name the questions that must be answered before work moves forward. A behavior change needs business review. An OpenAPI or persistence change needs technical review. A routine, reversible edit may need only a short recorded decision and focused verification.

16.5 Scale the artifacts to the risk

OpenSpec is a control for uncertainty and coordination. It is not a requirement that every line change receive the same amount of ceremony. Before creating a full change, classify the work by its uncertainty, reversibility, and blast radius.

Use a lighter record for a routine, low-risk, reversible change whose behavior is already covered by the accepted specifications. Use a full proposal, delta specification, design, task list, and verification record when the change crosses a repository boundary, changes a contract, modifies accepted behavior, or creates a meaningful regression risk. Urgency does not remove the need to record a decision. It may justify a smaller initial artifact and a follow-up review.

Record deliberate exceptions. A short note should state why the full workflow was unnecessary and what evidence still supports the change. This gives the next reviewer a reason for the missing artifacts instead of making the repository look incomplete by accident.

16.6 Maintain the context that agents use

The workflow itself changes over time. OpenSpec versions, Copilot integration files, generated clients, commands, and test tooling can all make an old instruction inaccurate. Treat those files as maintained engineering assets.

Review the repository context when you upgrade OpenSpec or the Copilot integration. Remove duplicated instructions when two files begin to disagree. Check command examples against the versions used by the repositories. Revisit generated integration files after an update rather than assuming that a new version preserves the old layout or behavior.

The same rule applies to the book’s examples. A command that worked for the prepared repositories at one version can become misleading after a tool upgrade. Keep version-sensitive guidance tied to a tested version, and make the version visible when a reader needs it to reproduce the result.

16.7 Capstone: defend the amount of specification

Complete the capstone by proposing a new commercial truck search change across both repositories. The purpose is not to produce the largest possible set of artifacts. It is to defend the amount of specification work the change needs.

  1. State the business goal, affected behavior, non-goals, and owner.
  2. Classify the change by uncertainty, reversibility, urgency, and blast radius.
  3. Choose the OpenSpec artifacts that follow from that classification.
  4. Record the backend contract boundary and the frontend impact, including generated-client work.
  5. Assign business and technical review where the change requires those decisions.
  6. Link the Jira issue, OpenSpec change, commits, pull requests, and verification evidence.
  7. Apply, verify, synchronize, and archive the change only after the accepted specifications and implementation agree.

16.8 Verification checkpoint

Ask a reviewer in a fresh session to reconstruct the capstone’s intent and evidence using repository artifacts alone. The reviewer should be able to identify the business decision, the changed behavior, the responsible reviewers, the contract boundary, and the evidence for completion without opening the originating chat session.

If the reviewer cannot do that, find the missing decision or link before treating the change as a team practice success. The problem may be an underspecified requirement, an unrecorded exception, a stale instruction, or a verification step that produced no recoverable evidence.

16.9 What you learned

SDD works as an engineering control when the team records intent, assigns human ownership, scales the artifacts to the risk, and preserves evidence through synchronization and archive. It also has a cost: someone must review the decisions, maintain the context, and keep the artifacts current. A proportionate workflow makes that cost visible instead of hiding it in repeated rework or fragile agent sessions.
